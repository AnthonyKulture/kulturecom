Google does not penalize **AI content** because an AI wrote it. It penalizes pages mass-produced to manipulate rankings without giving the reader anything, which its policy calls *scaled content abuse*, and it applies that policy in exactly the same way to text written by a person.

For a small business in Nice, Cannes or Monaco wondering whether to let ChatGPT write its pages, that changes how you should work. Below: the exact wording of the policy, what recent studies measure, the practices that actually put a site at risk, and how I advise using AI safely for both SEO and GEO.

## Does Google penalize AI-generated content?

No, not as such. Google said so in February 2023 in a [Search Central](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2023/02/google-search-and-ai-content) post: its systems aim to reward original, high-quality content, however it is produced. The same post adds that using automation, AI included, primarily to manipulate rankings violates its spam policies.

Its help page on [using generative AI content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/using-gen-ai-content) says the same. AI can help with research and with structuring original content. Generating many pages without adding value may count as scaled content abuse. And because a model predicts likely word sequences instead of checking facts, Google asks you to review everything before publishing, including titles, meta descriptions, structured data and image alt text.

## What is scaled content abuse, exactly?

**Scaled content abuse** is defined in [Google's spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies) as generating many pages for the primary purpose of manipulating search rankings and not helping users. It usually means large amounts of unoriginal content, "no matter how it's created".

Google gives five examples:

| Practice Google lists | What it looks like on the Riviera |
|---|---|
| Using generative AI to produce many pages without adding value | 80 "best restaurant in…" posts produced in one afternoon |
| Scraping feeds or search results, including through automated rewording or **translation** | A Cannes villa rental site that copies listings and runs them through a translator into six languages |
| Stitching together content from other pages without adding value | A "Monaco guide" patched together from three competitors |
| Running several sites to hide the volume | A network of mini-sites like "locksmith-nice", "locksmith-antibes"… |
| Pages that make no sense to a reader but are full of keywords | Lists of neighbourhoods and queries with no useful sentence |

The policy dates from the March 2024 update, when Google widened its old automated content policy to target mass production, whether by automation, humans or both. When the rollout finished, Google reported on April 26, 2024 that its results showed [45% less low-quality, unoriginal content](https://blog.google/products/search/google-search-update-march-2024/), an internal figure no third party has verified.

A related policy often catches local businesses: *doorway abuse*. Among its examples, Google lists pages targeted at specific regions or cities that funnel users to one page. A "plumber Cagnes-sur-Mer" page identical to the "plumber Saint-Laurent-du-Var" page apart from the town name risks being treated as a doorway, whether or not an AI wrote it.

## What happens if a site is hit?

Google detects spam with automated systems and, when needed, human review that can lead to a **manual action**. Its policies say a site that breaks them may rank lower or not appear at all, and that for attempts to get around the rules Google may remove whole sections of the site.

A manual action shows up in Search Console, in the Manual actions report. According to [Search Console Help](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/9044175), it can affect part of the site or the whole site. To get out, you fix every affected page, then request a review explaining the problem and the fixes. Reviews take from a few days to a few weeks.

The 2026 calendar shows the topic is still live. Google's official Search Status Dashboard logged four spam updates this year (late March, late June, mid-August, then late September through October 7), and the November 2026 core update finished on November 24.

## What do studies say about AI and rankings?

Two Ahrefs studies give a sense of what happens in the results. Both rely on an AI detector, so on estimates, as the authors point out themselves.

| Study | Sample | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| [Ahrefs, July 2025](https://ahrefs.com/blog/ai-generated-content-does-not-hurt-your-google-rankings/) | 600,000 top-20 pages for 100,000 keywords | 4.6% fully AI, 13.5% fully human, 81.9% mixed; correlation of 0.011 between AI share and position, effectively zero |
| [Ahrefs, July 2026](https://ahrefs.com/blog/google-doesnt-punish-ai-content/) | Top-10 pages for 100,000 queries (June 2026) | 82.2% of top-3 pages contain less than 50% AI; low and moderate AI pages get 2 to 3 times more impressions |

The second study adds a useful number: 49.28% of low-AI pages were indexed, against 40.35% of very high AI pages. Its author, Ryan Law, concludes that Google targets bad content rather than AI, and that heavily AI pages are more often poor.

That matches what I see. A fully generated page can rank, but it starts at a disadvantage, because it usually says what the other nine results already say.

## What about GEO: ChatGPT, Perplexity and AI Overviews?

GEO, or *Generative Engine Optimization*, is about getting cited in AI answers. On Google's side, the rule is the same as for rankings. Its [guide to optimizing for AI features](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide) says AI Overviews and AI Mode rely on both the core ranking systems and the spam-fighting systems, and that mass-producing pages to manipulate generative AI responses violates the scaled content policy.

The same guide contrasts "commodity" content, such as generic tip lists, with content that offers expert or first-hand takes beyond common knowledge. It also notes that a high number of pages does not make a site higher quality.

OpenAI and Perplexity have not published an equivalent rule on AI content. Their citation logic is close, though: an answer engine cites the source that gives a precise fact. A generic piece on "the best time to visit Nice" looks like a thousand others, while a table of your real airport shuttle times gives the AI something to quote.

## How should you use AI for your content?

Google asks three questions on its [helpful content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) page: who created the content, how, and why. "Why" is perhaps the most important, Google writes: if a page exists mainly to attract search visits, it starts badly. Google also notes it has no preferred word count.

From there, here is my method:

1. Start from a fact only you have. Your prices, your turnaround times, the questions customers ask on the phone, the neighbourhoods you actually cover. AI rewords; it cannot make up your trade.
2. Use AI for structure and the first draft, not for numbers. Every price, date or statistic gets checked at the source.
3. Review everything Google reads: title, meta description, Schema.org markup, alt text. That is Google's own instruction, and it is where mistakes slip through.
4. Add a real byline. Google encourages accurate authorship information and warns against fake profiles, AI-generated headshots included.
5. Translate with a human review. Many Riviera sites run in English, Italian or Russian. An unreviewed machine translation of scraped pages is one of Google's examples; a reviewed translation of your own pages is fine. The guide to [multilingual websites on the French Riviera](/en/blog/multilingual-website-french-riviera/) covers the right structure.
6. Build a page per town only if it has something of its own: your jobs there, local constraints, your response time. Otherwise one service-area page beats fifteen copies.
7. Keep a human pace. Ten solid pages do more for a Monaco firm than two hundred pages generated in a week.

| AI use | SEO risk | My view |
|---|---|---|
| Outlines, question ideas, rewording text you wrote | None | Do it |
| First draft reviewed, corrected and filled with your facts | Low | Do it, checking every number |
| Reviewed translation of your own pages | Low | Do it, with hreflang |
| Town pages identical apart from the name | High (doorway) | Avoid |
| Dozens of posts published without review | High (scaled content) | Avoid |

## Frequently asked questions

### Can Google tell that ChatGPT wrote a text?

Google does not need to know to apply its policy. The policy targets pages mass-produced without value for readers, "no matter how it's created", and a sloppy human text falls under it too.

### Should you disclose that an article was written with AI?

Google recommends it when a reader might reasonably wonder how the content was produced. It does not require it for rankings, and it advises against giving AI an author byline.

### How many AI articles can you publish safely?

Google gives no threshold. It judges the purpose and value of pages, not their number, and says a high quantity of pages does not make a site more relevant.

### Does AI content stop you from being cited by ChatGPT?

OpenAI has published no rule that excludes it. In practice, a generic text is unlikely to be cited, because it offers no fact you cannot find elsewhere.
